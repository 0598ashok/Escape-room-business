Introduction

When a marriage ends in New York, one of the most legally complex aspects is determining how marital assets will be divided. Unlike community property states where assets are split 50/50, New York follows the doctrine of equitable distribution — meaning the court divides marital property in a manner it deems fair and equitable, though not necessarily equal.

Understanding equitable distribution is critical for anyone entering or navigating divorce proceedings. As an attorney who has handled hundreds of high-asset divorce cases over two decades, I've seen how this process can be both clarifying and surprising for clients unfamiliar with its nuances.

What Is Marital Property?

Before a court can divide property, it must first distinguish between marital and separate property. Marital property generally includes all assets acquired during the marriage, regardless of which spouse's name is on the title. This can include:

  • Real estate purchased during the marriage
  • Retirement accounts and pension benefits accumulated during marriage
  • Business interests and professional practices built during marriage
  • Marital savings, investments, and brokerage accounts

Separate property — assets owned before marriage, or received as gifts or inheritance during marriage — is generally not subject to equitable distribution. However, this classification can become complicated when separate and marital assets are commingled.

"The line between separate and marital property is often blurry in practice. Proper documentation and legal counsel are essential to protecting what is rightfully yours."

Factors Courts Consider

New York Domestic Relations Law §236(B) outlines numerous factors courts weigh when determining equitable distribution. Key considerations include the duration of the marriage, the age and health of both spouses, and the future financial circumstances of each party.
